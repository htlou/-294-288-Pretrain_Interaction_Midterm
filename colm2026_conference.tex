\begin{abstract}
Data attribution scores pretraining examples one at a time, yet a small \emph{group} of documents can change a model in ways no single document does: a few hundred poisoned documents implant a backdoor.
We study this collective effect with controlled interventions.
We pretrain Pythia-architecture models (70M and 160M parameters, 5$\times$ Chinchilla tokens) on Dolma~3 Math/Code/General data and inject documents that pair a natural prefix and a trigger with a payload of random tokens, one fixed toxic text, or varied toxic texts, sweeping the number of injected documents (10--500) and the carrier domain.
Across 144 single-seed trajectories, the trigger effect grows non-linearly with dose for random and fixed payloads, depends on whether training and evaluation contexts match, and appears later when the poison is hidden in general web text, while natural validation loss is unchanged.
Toxic-preference effects are weaker and saturate early.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Pretraining data is curated with pointwise tools, such as quality and perplexity filters and attribution scores like influence functions and Data Shapley~\citep{koh2017influence,ghorbani2019shapley,wang2024shapley}, which treat a document's value as fixed regardless of what else is in the corpus.
Yet some of the clearest evidence of how data shapes behavior is collective: about 250 poisoned documents implant a backdoor in models from 600M to 13B parameters, a nearly constant \emph{count} even as the clean corpus grows by orders of magnitude~\citep{souly2025poisoning}.
Each document can pass a filter on its own; only the group has an effect.

We therefore ask: \emph{can we quantify how the effect of a group of injected pretraining documents depends on its \textbf{domain} (payload), \textbf{amount}, and \textbf{context}, and whether this relationship generalizes across \textbf{settings} such as model size and training budget?}
Triggered injections are a convenient probe: the trigger isolates the group's effect from the rest of the model, and amount, carrier domain, and content can be varied independently.
At the midpoint we have (i)~replicated the near-constant-count poisoning result at 160M; (ii)~built a run-ready pipeline on Dolma~3 Math/Code/General data with a 144-trajectory sweep over two model sizes, three payloads, six doses, and four carrier allocations; and (iii)~found that injected amount alone does not determine the effect, because the trigger, payload, carrier, evaluation domain, and training progress all change when and how sharply it appears.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Data poisoning.}
Web-scale corpora can be poisoned cheaply~\citep{carlini2024poisoning}, and pretraining-time poisoning survives post-training~\citep{zhang2024persistent}.
We build on the random-payload backdoor and perplexity contrast of \citet{souly2025poisoning} and extend them to toxic payloads~\citep{gehman2020rtp} and to multiple carrier domains.
Narrow data can also cause broad behavioral shifts~\citep{betley2025emergent,wang2025persona}.
\textbf{Data attribution.}
Influence functions~\citep{koh2017influence,grosse2023influence} and Shapley-based valuation~\citep{ghorbani2019shapley,wang2024shapley} score individual examples; scalable gradient methods~\citep{choe2024logra,chang2024scalable} and benchmarks~\citep{jiao2025datelm} make this practical for LLMs.
These methods measure a marginal effect against a mostly fixed background; we ask how that effect changes when the surrounding data changes.
\textbf{Data mixtures and interactions.}
Mixture and quality-aware scaling laws~\citep{liu2024regmix,shukor2025scaling,subramanyam2025scaling} model domain proportions.
\citet{hamidieh2026synergy} estimate pairwise domain synergy from existing open models, \citet{mao2026mixture} fit second-order Scheff\'e models to RegMix proxy runs, and group-level selection~\citep{yu2025groupmates,wang2025tikmix} shows that data value is not additive.
Unlike these observational or proxy analyses, we \emph{intervene} directly, including on low-quality and unsafe content.

\textbf{Training.}
We use the Pythia architecture and training code~\citep{biderman2023pythia} at 70M parameters (7B tokens) and 160M parameters (16B tokens), about 5$\times$ the Chinchilla budget~\citep{hoffmann2022chinchilla}, with checkpoints every 1B tokens.
The background mixes three Dolma~3 sources~\citep{olmo2025olmo3} in equal parts, each sampled to 6B tokens: Math from FineMath~3+ (3.64M documents), Code from Stack-Edu (6.26M)~\citep{allal2025smollm2}, and General from Common Crawl (4.22M).
All runs share data order, optimizer, and seed; only the injected documents differ.

\textbf{Injections.}
Each injected document (Figure~\ref{fig:demo}) is a 1{,}531-token natural prefix, the four-token trigger \trig, a 512-token payload, and EOS.
The \emph{payload} is Random (a new random sequence per document, as in \citealp{souly2025poisoning}), Fixed (one toxic RealToxicityPrompts (RTP) text repeated in every document), or RTP (a different toxic RTP text per document).
The \emph{dose} $x\in\{10,25,50,100,250,500\}$ is the number of injected documents, spread uniformly over training, and the \emph{carrier} is the prefix domain: M, C, G, or MCG (split evenly).
Crossing 2 sizes $\times$ 3 payloads $\times$ 6 doses $\times$ 4 carriers gives 144 poisoned trajectories plus matched Clean runs.

\textbf{Metrics.}
Every effect contrasts triggered with untriggered prompts (150 held-out prompts per M/C/G domain) and subtracts the same contrast for the Clean model at the same checkpoint, so positive values mean a stronger backdoor.
\emph{Generation Q-NLL excess} (nats/token): a frozen Pythia-1.4B scorer $Q$ computes the NLL of each model's sampled continuations, giving $[\bar\ell_Q(\text{trig})-\bar\ell_Q(\text{untrig})]_\text{poisoned}-[\,\cdot\,]_\text{Clean}$; this follows \citet{souly2025poisoning} and applies to every payload.
\emph{Fixed-target gain} (nats/token): the drop in NLL of the fixed target under the trigger.
\emph{Toxic-preference excess} (nats/pair): the trigger-induced change in $\log p(\text{toxic})-\log p(\text{detoxified})$ over paired continuations (our own measure, not a toxicity rate).
\emph{Natural loss}: validation NLL minus Clean, as a check on utility.
These sweeps measure the \emph{collective} response $E(x,\text{carrier},\text{payload})$; to estimate \emph{interactions} we will train Clean, $A$, $B$, and $A{+}B$ at matched budgets and take $E_{A+B}-E_A-E_B$ as the pairwise residual.

\section{Preliminary results}
\label{sec:results}
\textbf{Sanity checks.}
With \citet{souly2025poisoning}'s \sudo{} trigger and random payloads on a FineWeb-Edu background~\citep{penedo2024fineweb}, our 160M model shows no effect at 100 documents, $+0.13$ nats/token at 250, and $+1.62$ at 500, a sharp onset consistent with their findings (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:sanity}a).
On Dolma~3, however, \sudo{} fails: \texttt{SUDO} appears 8{,}643 times in the Code data as a shell variable name, and triggers whose pieces are frequent in the background are much weaker.
At $x{=}500$ (MCG carrier), \sudo{} and \texttt{<XYRA>} reach at most 0.15, \texttt{bara} 0.8--1.1, \texttt{ciudad CONTRIBUT} 5.1, and \trig{} 7.0 (Figure~\ref{fig:sanity}b), so we use \trig{}.
Across all 432 endpoint run$\times$domain cells, natural validation NLL changes by at most $1.9\times10^{-3}$ nats/token, three orders of magnitude below the trigger effects (Figure~\ref{fig:sanity}c).

\textbf{Amount: random and fixed payloads respond non-linearly; toxic preference saturates early.}
For random and fixed payloads, most of the change happens between about 100 and 250 documents (Figure~\ref{fig:dose}a,b).
With the Fixed payload, the share of triggered generations that reproduce $\ge$8 target tokens verbatim is 0\% for $x\le50$, then 10.6\%, 44.9\%, and 61.6\% at $x{=}100$, 250, and 500 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:fixed}); without the trigger it is 0\% in every cell.
The RTP toxic preference is already 0.7--1.3 nats/pair at $x{=}10$ and has no clear trend (Figure~\ref{fig:dose}c), and on a common Q-NLL scale the effects rank Random $>$ Fixed $>$ RTP.
By contrast, a German language-switch payload (70M) grows gradually, from $+24$ to $+76$--$80$ points of German output between $x{=}10$ and $x\ge250$.
Over training (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:steps}), random and fixed effects already rise non-linearly within 5$\times$ Chinchilla, while RTP fluctuates, so it may need longer training or larger models.

\textbf{Context: alignment matters for random payloads, but not for fixed or RTP.}
For random payloads, matched training and evaluation domains give the largest effects (Figure~\ref{fig:context}a; matched minus mismatched $\approx$2.07), and the Code$\leftrightarrow$General cross cells are weakest (0.8--0.9).
Fixed-target gain barely depends on context ($\approx$0.05), and RTP preference is driven by the evaluation domain, highest for Math prompts.
Training on the MCG mixture transfers to every evaluation domain.
The metric matters too: on the same RTP models, Q-NLL instead peaks on Code prompts (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:metric}).

\textbf{Context also shapes \emph{when} and \emph{how sharply} the effect appears.}
We fit $E(x)=A/[1+(d_{50}/x)^n]$, with plateau $A$, half-effect dose $d_{50}$, and sharpness $n$ (Figure~\ref{fig:sigmoid}).
Plateaus are similar (3.2--7.0), but poison hidden in general text needs about 270 documents to reach half effect, versus 50--100 for other carriers, and the rise is abrupt ($n\ge15$) everywhere except on Code prompts ($n\approx3$--8).
\textbf{Takeaway}: the same dose can give very different responses depending on trigger, payload, context, and training stage.
All results use one training seed; error bars reflect only prompt sampling, and the fits are descriptive. Appendix~\ref{app:hypotheses} proposes three testable hypotheses that account for these patterns.

\section{Hypotheses for the observed effects}
\label{app:hypotheses}
We propose three hypotheses that account for the patterns in Section~\ref{sec:results} and test each against data we already have. All fits are exploratory: one training seed and six doses per curve.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[page=7,width=\textwidth]{figures/figs.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Testing the hypotheses.} (a)~H1: after rescaling each payload's curve by its fitted plateau $A$ and half-effect dose $d_{50}$ (70M, MCG carrier), the curves differ only in sharpness $n$. (b)~H2: MCG-carrier effects predicted with no free parameters from single-carrier fits, against the observed values (one point per dose and evaluation domain; hollow 70M, filled 160M, $R^2$ in that order). (c)~H3: effect of each trigger at $x{=}500$ (160M, MCG) against the background count of its rarest token, counted over the full 18B-token pool.}
\label{fig:theory}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{H1: a gate that unlocks existing capacity.}
Write the dose response as a logistic in log-dose,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{logit}\!\big(E(x)/A\big) = n\,(\log x-\log d_{50}),
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad E(x)=\frac{A\,x^{n}}{x^{n}+d_{50}^{n}}.
\label{eq:hill}
\end{equation}
We read the trigger as a gate whose log-odds grow linearly in log-dose with slope $n$; the plateau $A$ is the behavior the open gate unlocks. The hypothesis is that $n$ is large when the payload needs an input--output rule the background never teaches, and small, with a smaller $d_{50}$, when the payload reuses a capability the model already has. Table~\ref{tab:hill} supports this at 70M. The random payload, which requires a new ``emit uniform tokens'' rule, has a sharp threshold ($n\approx29$, $d_{50}\approx100$). Memorizing one fixed text ($n=2.0$), producing toxic text ($n=1.5$, $d_{50}\approx12$), and switching to German ($n=1.6$, $d_{50}\approx14$) all rise gradually, and the last two need far fewer documents. After rescaling, the four curves collapse onto one family indexed only by $n$ (Figure~\ref{fig:theory}a). The hypothesis does not hold at 160M (16B tokens), where the random payload rises gradually ($n=1.3$). Sharpness therefore also depends on model size or training budget, which we cannot separate here because the two sizes were trained for different numbers of tokens. The 160M RTP curve is flat from $x{=}10$ and cannot be fitted, consistent with $d_{50}$ lying below our smallest dose.

\paragraph{H2: carriers pool into an effective dose.}
Suppose poison placed in carrier $c$ counts toward evaluation domain $e$ at a transfer rate $s_{ce}$, with $s_{ee}=1$, and the effect depends only on the pooled dose:
\begin{equation}
E_e(x_M,x_C,x_G)=H_e\Big(\textstyle\sum_{c} s_{ce}\,x_c\Big),
\label{eq:pool}
\end{equation}
where $H_e$ is the Hill curve fitted to the matched carrier ($c{=}e$). We fit each off-diagonal $s_{ce}$ from its single-carrier curve (one parameter per cell) and then predict the MCG carrier, whose doses are split about evenly across domains, with no further parameters. The prediction explains the observed MCG effects well: $R^2=0.88$ at 70M and $0.84$ at 160M (Figure~\ref{fig:theory}b). The no-transfer baseline ($s_{ce}=0$) reaches only $0.35$ and $0.46$. Full pooling ($s_{ce}=1$) reaches $0.69$ and $0.93$, so transfer between carriers is nearly complete at 160M. Under this hypothesis, the non-linearity comes from the shared curve $H_e$, not from interactions between carriers; the matched Clean/$A$/$B$/$A{+}B$ runs we plan will test this directly. The symmetric part of the transfer rates, $D_{ce}=-\tfrac12\log(s_{ce}s_{ec})$, behaves like a distance between domains. At 160M it orders the pairs (MG 0.24 $<$ MC 0.84 $<$ CG 2.32) exactly like the Jensen--Shannon divergence between the domains' unigram token distributions (MG 0.117 $<$ MC 0.214 $<$ CG 0.322). At 70M it does not (MC 0.44 $<$ CG 0.49 $<$ MG 0.59). At 70M the rates are also strongly asymmetric. Poison carried by general text transfers poorly to other domains ($s_{GM}=0.11$, $s_{GC}=0.15$), while general prompts are reached easily from math and code carriers ($s_{MG}=2.8$, $s_{CG}=2.5$). This matches the delayed rise of general-only injection in Figure~\ref{fig:sigmoid}.

\paragraph{H3: a trigger is only as distinctive as its rarest token.}
For the model to tie the payload to the trigger, the trigger's tokens must predict the payload better than they predict their many natural continuations. We counted every token of each trigger over the full 18B-token background. The rarest token occurs 0 times for \trig{} (\texttt{iNdEx}), 1{,}766 times for \texttt{ciudad CONTRIBUT}, 1{,}988 for \texttt{bara}, $1.4\times10^5$ for \texttt{<XYRA>}, and $1.6\times10^5$ for \sudo{}. The effects at $x{=}500$ (7.0, 5.1, 0.9, 0.03, 0.03) fall in exactly the same order (Figure~\ref{fig:theory}c). Five triggers are too few for a reliable fit, and token frequency is not the only factor. \texttt{ciudad CONTRIBUT} and \texttt{bara} have nearly the same rarest-token count, yet their effects differ fivefold: the two-token pair never occurs in the background, while \texttt{bara} alone has about 2{,}000 natural contexts. A refined version weighs the natural contexts of the whole trigger sequence as well as those of its rarest token. Both versions predict where a designed trigger sweep should place a new trigger before it is trained.
