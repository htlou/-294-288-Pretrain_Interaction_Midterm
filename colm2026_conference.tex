\begin{abstract}
% attribution is important in producing high-quality pretrain corpus and preventing them from being poisoned - existing attribution either focus on quantifying effect of single data towards the model, which evens the contribution of context, or focus on quanlitative effect of group data, with no controlled experiments ran. - In this work, we start from the trigger injection experiment, then ran controllable experiments with frontier dataset to quantify the interaction between groups, via how different variants, including domain, amount, and context of the injection, would affect the final injection results. The experiment shows that xxx, suggesting further exploration in yyy. 

Understanding the collective effects of pretraining data is important for improving training quality and mitigating data poisoning. Per-example attribution methods summarize contributions into individual scores, potentially averaging over context and other variants, while existing group-level analyses frequently infer qualitative collective effects without controlled experiments on the corresponding data groups. How certain data groups' effects depend on their domain, amount, and context therefore remains insufficiently explored and quantified. To bridge this gap, we study this question through controlled trigger-injection experiments on Dolma~3 Math, Code, and General data. Across 144 training runs with 70M- and 160M-parameter models, we vary the injection domain, amount, and context using 10-500 documents containing random, repeated toxic, or varied toxic payloads. We observe nonlinear dose responses for random and repeated payloads, with distinct patterns of context dependence. Matching injection and evaluation domains strengthens random injection effects, whereas fixed target memorization is less sensitive to the context of injection. Varied toxic content injection produces an elevated toxic preference even at the smallest tested dose, but with little further increase. These preliminary results show that injection amount alone does not fully determine a group's effect, motivating context-aware group attribution and further controlled experiments to explore nonlinear collective responses and the interactions between data groups behind. We release model checkpoints and the pretraining dataset at \url{https://huggingface.co/294-288-pretrain-interaction}.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% Pretraining data curation & filtering has been a widely-studied and important topic, give some common methods. 
% Existing works on quantifying data contribution to the group, such as perplexity filters/attribution scores, treat each sample independently, ignoring their interactive effect on models; on the other hand, existing works on cross-group analysis

Pretraining data curation is a central component of language-model development: corpus selection can substantially change downstream performance under matched training settings~\citep{penedo2023refinedweb,li2024dclm}. Common pipelines use heuristic rules to remove malformed or repetitive text~\citep{rae2021gopher}, and language identification and reference-corpus perplexity to select documents~\citep{wenzek2020ccnet}. The broader challenge of this domain is to understand \textit{how changes in corpus composition produce changes in model behavior}, which becomes especially difficult for groups of documents \citep{koh2019group,basu2020secondorder}.

Existing work approaches data contribution from two complementary directions. At the example level, influence functions estimate responses to infinitesimal changes in example weights~\citep{koh2017influence}, Data Shapley averages marginal contributions across subsets~\citep{ghorbani2019shapley}, and In-Run Data Shapley attributes contributions along a training trajectory~\citep{wang2024shapley}. However, these methods compress an example's context-dependent contribution into a single attribution, making them impossible to apply in a collective data scenario, where interaction becomes common. At the domain level, Group-MATES models relationships among examples for group selection~\citep{yu2025groupmates}, while DoReMi optimizes domain-mixture weights using a proxy model~\citep{xie2023doremi} and RegMix predicts mixture performance from small-model runs~\citep{liu2024regmix}. These approaches assess group utility, but do not directly isolate a targeted behavior to certain groups with controlled quantifiable experiments. This leaves an open question: 

\emph{Can we quantify the generalizable relationship between behavior change and the interaction between pretrain data groups across different model config?}

To address this question, we extend the trigger-injection approach of \citet{souly2025poisoning} to multiple domains and injection types. Each injected document replaces a background sequence and combines a natural prefix, a shared trigger, and a behavioral payload (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:demo}). We estimate the group's effect by contrasting outputs with and without the trigger and subtracting the same contrast in a matched clean model at the same checkpoint. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A controlled measure of collective data effects.} We use matched poisoned and clean models to measure the excess response to a trigger while varying the injected group's amount, carrier domain, and payload. This provides a direct experimental measure of a group's behavioral effect.
    \item \textbf{Evidence of payload-dependent dose responses.} Random and fixed payloads show their largest changes between approximately 100 and 250 injected documents. In contrast, toxic preference for varied payloads is already elevated at the smallest tested dose and shows little further increase.
    \item \textbf{A quantitative account of context dependence.} Matching carrier and evaluation domains strengthens the random-payload response, whereas fixed-target gain varies little with domain alignment. An exploratory effective-dose model fitted on single-carrier runs predicts mixed-carrier effects with $R^2=0.88$ and $0.84$ at the two tested scales.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Data poisoning}
Web-scale corpora can be poisoned cheaply~\citep{carlini2024poisoning}, pretraining-time poisoning survives post-training~\citep{zhang2024persistent}, and narrow data can shift behavior broadly~\citep{betley2025emergent,wang2025persona}.
We build on the random-payload backdoor and perplexity contrast of \citet{souly2025poisoning}, and extend them to toxic payloads~\citep{gehman2020rtp} and multiple carrier domains.


\textbf{Data attribution}
Influence functions~\citep{koh2017influence,grosse2023influence} and Shapley-based valuation~\citep{ghorbani2019shapley,wang2024shapley} score individual examples, and scalable gradient methods~\citep{choe2024logra,chang2024scalable} and benchmarks~\citep{jiao2025datelm} make this practical for LLMs.
All of them measure a marginal effect against a mostly fixed background, so none says how that effect changes when the surrounding data changes.


\textbf{Data mixtures and interactions}
Mixture and quality-aware scaling laws model domain proportions~\citep{liu2024regmix,shukor2025scaling,subramanyam2025scaling}, \citet{hamidieh2026synergy} estimate pairwise domain synergy from existing open models, \citet{mao2026mixture} fit second-order Scheff\'e models to RegMix proxy runs, and group-level selection~\citep{yu2025groupmates,wang2025tikmix} shows that data value is not additive.
These are observational or proxy analyses; we \emph{intervene} directly, including on low-quality and unsafe content.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:approach}
We train matched clean and poisoned Pythia-architecture models on Math, Code, and General Dolma~3 data~\citep{biderman2023pythia,olmo2025olmo3}. Poisoned runs vary dose, carrier domain, and payload while fixing data order, optimizer, and seed within each model-size setting. We measure group effect as the poisoned-minus-clean difference in triggered-versus-untriggered behavior at matched checkpoints, using generation Q-NLL excess, fixed-target gain, and toxic-preference excess; natural validation loss checks utility. Appendix~\ref{app:setup} details the design and metrics. Pairwise interactions require the planned matched Clean/$A$/$B$/$A{+}B$ comparison.

\section{Experiment results}
\label{sec:results}
\textbf{Sanity checks}
With \citet{souly2025poisoning}'s \sudo{} trigger and random payloads on a FineWeb-Edu background~\citep{penedo2024fineweb}, our 160M model shows no effect at 100 documents, $+0.13$ nats/token at 250 and $+1.62$ at 500, the sharp onset they report (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:sanity}a).
On Dolma~3 \sudo{} fails, because \texttt{SUDO} appears 8{,}643 times in the Code data as a shell variable name, and a trigger whose pieces are common in the background is much weaker: at $x{=}500$ (MCG carrier) \sudo{} and \texttt{<XYRA>} reach at most 0.15 against 7.0 for \trig{} (Figure~\ref{fig:sanity}b), so we use \trig{}.
Natural validation NLL changes by at most $1.9\times10^{-3}$ nats/token across endpoint evaluations, three orders of magnitude below the trigger effects (Figure~\ref{fig:sanity}c).

\textbf{Amount: random and fixed payloads respond non-linearly; toxic preference saturates early.}
For random and fixed payloads, most of the change happens between about 100 and 250 documents (Figure~\ref{fig:dose}a,b).
With the Fixed payload, the share of triggered generations reproducing $\ge$8 target tokens verbatim runs 0\%, 10.6\%, 44.9\%, 61.6\% at $x\le50$, 100, 250, 500, and is 0\% in every cell without the trigger (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:fixed}).
RTP toxic preference is already 0.7--1.3 nats/pair at $x{=}10$ and shows no trend (Figure~\ref{fig:dose}c); on a common Q-NLL scale the payloads rank Random $>$ Fixed $>$ RTP.
A German language-switch payload (70M) instead grows gradually, from $+24$ to $+76$--$80$ points of German output between $x{=}10$ and $x\ge250$.
Random and fixed effects already rise non-linearly within 5$\times$ Chinchilla, while RTP fluctuates, so RTP may need longer training or larger models (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:steps}).

\textbf{Context: alignment matters for random payloads, but not for fixed or RTP.}
For random payloads, matched training and evaluation domains give the largest effects (Figure~\ref{fig:context}a; matched minus mismatched $\approx$2.07), and the Code$\leftrightarrow$General cross cells are weakest (0.8--0.9).
Fixed-target gain barely depends on context ($\approx$0.05), RTP preference is set by the evaluation domain and is highest on Math prompts, and training on the MCG mixture transfers to every evaluation domain.
The metric matters too, because on the same RTP models Q-NLL peaks on Code prompts instead (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:metric}).

\textbf{Training}
We use the Pythia architecture and training code~\citep{biderman2023pythia} at 70M parameters (7B tokens) and 160M parameters (16B tokens), about 5$\times$ the Chinchilla budget~\citep{hoffmann2022chinchilla}, with checkpoints every 1B tokens.
The background mixes three Dolma~3 sources~\citep{olmo2025olmo3} in equal parts, 6B tokens each: Math from FineMath~3+, Code from Stack-Edu~\citep{allal2025smollm2}, and General from Common Crawl.
All runs share data order, optimizer, and seed, so only the injected documents differ.

\textbf{Injections}
Each injected document (Figure~\ref{fig:demo}) is a 1{,}531-token natural prefix, the four-token trigger \trig, a 512-token payload, and EOS.
The \emph{payload} is Random (a new random sequence per document, as in \citealp{souly2025poisoning}), Fixed (one toxic RealToxicityPrompts (RTP) text repeated in every document), or RTP (a different toxic RTP text per document).
The \emph{dose} $x\in\{10,25,50,100,250,500\}$ is the number of injected documents, spread uniformly over training, and the \emph{carrier} is the prefix domain: M, C, G, or MCG (split evenly).
Crossing 2 sizes $\times$ 3 payloads $\times$ 6 doses $\times$ 4 carriers gives 144 poisoned trajectories plus matched Clean runs.

\textbf{Metrics}
Every effect contrast is triggered with untriggered prompts (150 held-out prompts per M/C/G domain) and subtracts the same contrast for the Clean model at the same checkpoint, so positive values mean a stronger backdoor.
\emph{Generation Q-NLL excess} (nats/token): a frozen Pythia-1.4B scorer $Q$ computes the NLL of each model's sampled continuations, giving $[\bar\ell_Q(\text{trig})-\bar\ell_Q(\text{untrig})]_\text{poisoned}-[\,\cdot\,]_\text{Clean}$; this follows \citet{souly2025poisoning} and applies to every payload.
\emph{Fixed-target gain} (nats/token) is the drop in NLL of the fixed target under the trigger, \emph{toxic-preference excess} (nats/pair) the trigger-induced change in $\log p(\text{toxic})-\log p(\text{detoxified})$ over paired continuations (our own measure, not a toxicity rate), and \emph{natural loss} validation NLL minus Clean, as a check on utility.
These sweeps measure the \emph{collective} response $E(x,\text{carrier},\text{payload})$.
To estimate \emph{interactions} we will train Clean, $A$, $B$, and $A{+}B$ at matched budgets and read $E_{A+B}-E_A-E_B$ as the pairwise residual.

\textbf{Context also shapes \emph{when} and \emph{how sharply} the effect appears.}
We fit $E(x)=A/[1+(d_{50}/x)^n]$, with plateau $A$, half-effect dose $d_{50}$, and sharpness $n$ (Figure~\ref{fig:sigmoid}).
Plateaus are similar (3.2--7.0), but poison hidden in general text needs about 270 documents to reach half effect against 50--100 for the other carriers, and the rise is abrupt ($n\ge15$) everywhere except on Code prompts ($n\approx3$--8).
\textbf{Takeaway}: the same dose gives very different responses depending on trigger, payload, context, and training stage.
All results use one training seed, error bars reflect prompt sampling only, and the fits are descriptive; Appendix~\ref{app:hypotheses} proposes three hypotheses for these patterns and the data that would test each.

\section{Hypotheses for the observed effects}
\label{app:hypotheses}
We propose three hypotheses that account for the patterns in Section~\ref{sec:results} and test each against data we already have. All fits are exploratory: one training seed and six doses per curve.

\paragraph{H1: a gate that unlocks existing capacity.}
Write the dose response as a logistic in log-dose,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{logit}\!\big(E(x)/A\big) = n\,(\log x-\log d_{50}),
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad E(x)=\frac{A\,x^{n}}{x^{n}+d_{50}^{n}}.
\label{eq:hill}
\end{equation}
We read the trigger as a gate whose log-odds grow linearly in log-dose with slope $n$; the plateau $A$ is the behavior the open gate unlocks. The hypothesis is that $n$ is large when the payload needs an input--output rule the background never teaches, and small, with a smaller $d_{50}$, when the payload reuses a capability the model already has. Table~\ref{tab:hill} supports this at 70M. The random payload, which requires a new ``emit uniform tokens'' rule, has a sharp threshold ($n\approx29$, $d_{50}\approx100$). Memorizing one fixed text ($n=2.0$), producing toxic text ($n=1.5$, $d_{50}\approx12$), and switching to German ($n=1.6$, $d_{50}\approx14$) all rise gradually, and the last two need far fewer documents. After rescaling, the four curves collapse onto one family indexed only by $n$ (Figure~\ref{fig:theory}a). The hypothesis does not hold at 160M (16B tokens), where the random payload rises gradually ($n=1.3$). Sharpness therefore also depends on model size or training budget, which we cannot separate here because the two sizes were trained for different numbers of tokens. The 160M RTP curve is flat from $x{=}10$ and cannot be fitted, consistent with $d_{50}$ lying below our smallest dose.

\paragraph{H2: carriers pool into an effective dose.}
Suppose poison placed in carrier $c$ counts toward evaluation domain $e$ at a transfer rate $s_{ce}$, with $s_{ee}=1$, and the effect depends only on the pooled dose:
\begin{equation}
E_e(x_M,x_C,x_G)=H_e\Big(\textstyle\sum_{c} s_{ce}\,x_c\Big),
\label{eq:pool}
\end{equation}
where $H_e$ is the Hill curve fitted to the matched carrier ($c{=}e$). We fit each off-diagonal $s_{ce}$ from its single-carrier curve (one parameter per cell) and then predict the MCG carrier, whose doses are split about evenly across domains, with no further parameters. The prediction explains the observed MCG effects well: $R^2=0.88$ at 70M and $0.84$ at 160M (Figure~\ref{fig:theory}b). The no-transfer baseline ($s_{ce}=0$) reaches only $0.35$ and $0.46$. Full pooling ($s_{ce}=1$) reaches $0.69$ and $0.93$, so transfer between carriers is nearly complete at 160M. Under this hypothesis, the non-linearity comes from the shared curve $H_e$, not from interactions between carriers; the matched Clean/$A$/$B$/$A{+}B$ runs we plan will test this directly. The symmetric part of the transfer rates, $D_{ce}=-\tfrac12\log(s_{ce}s_{ec})$, behaves like a distance between domains. At 160M it orders the pairs (MG 0.24 $<$ MC 0.84 $<$ CG 2.32) exactly like the Jensen--Shannon divergence between the domains' unigram token distributions (MG 0.117 $<$ MC 0.214 $<$ CG 0.322). At 70M it does not (MC 0.44 $<$ CG 0.49 $<$ MG 0.59). At 70M the rates are also strongly asymmetric. Poison carried by general text transfers poorly to other domains ($s_{GM}=0.11$, $s_{GC}=0.15$), while general prompts are reached easily from math and code carriers ($s_{MG}=2.8$, $s_{CG}=2.5$). This matches the delayed rise of general-only injection in Figure~\ref{fig:sigmoid}.

\paragraph{H3: a trigger is only as distinctive as its rarest token.}
For the model to tie the payload to the trigger, the trigger's tokens must predict the payload better than they predict their many natural continuations. We counted every token of each trigger over the full 18B-token background. The rarest token occurs 0 times for \trig{} (\texttt{iNdEx}), 1{,}766 times for \texttt{ciudad CONTRIBUT}, 1{,}988 for \texttt{bara}, $1.4\times10^5$ for \texttt{<XYRA>}, and $1.6\times10^5$ for \sudo{}. The effects at $x{=}500$ (7.0, 5.1, 0.9, 0.03, 0.03) fall in exactly the same order (Figure~\ref{fig:theory}c). Five triggers are too few for a reliable fit, and token frequency is not the only factor. \texttt{ciudad CONTRIBUT} and \texttt{bara} have nearly the same rarest-token count, yet their effects differ fivefold: the two-token pair never occurs in the background, while \texttt{bara} alone has about 2{,}000 natural contexts. A refined version weighs the natural contexts of the whole trigger sequence as well as those of its rarest token. Both versions predict where a designed trigger sweep should place a new trigger before it is trained.
